What Atlas is, what is built, and what it commits to

A plain statement of what Atlas Identity does with a person's data today, what it cannot do by design, and where that is still a rule rather than a mathematical guarantee.

In one sentence

Atlas gives a person a portable identity that services can trust without a company standing behind it as a broker — and it is built so that Atlas itself cannot answer "where has this person been," even if compelled to try.

Contents 1. What Atlas Is 2. What Is Built Today 3. What Atlas Commits To 4. What Atlas Can and Cannot Answer 5. Where This Is a Rule, Not Yet a Guarantee 6. Questions and Review

01

What Atlas Is

Atlas is a portable, self-controlled identity: a way for a person to prove who they are to a service without that service — or Atlas — needing to hold a permanent, centralized record of them to do it.

Most services today recognize an account, not a person. Change devices, change networks, or lose access to one provider, and the relationship has to be rebuilt from nothing. Atlas is a credential rooted in something only the person holds — not a device, not a company account — so the identity survives a lost laptop or a changed carrier without becoming a new identity at every service that trusted the old one.

Atlas is not an open identity standard and not a general-purpose network product. It approves each service it works with individually, and it is not a substitute for a person's Internet provider — Atlas does not monitor, filter, or police what a person's connection reaches. That is deliberate, and explained in Section 3.

02

What Is Built Today

Atlas Identity is in closed testing, not public launch. What exists and has been run end to end:

A long-lived credential

Issued from a one-time recovery phrase the person holds, not from any single device. Losing a device does not change the identity a service sees.

Per-service identifiers

Each service Atlas connects to receives a different, fixed identifier for the same person — not one ID reused everywhere.

One verifying service

A demonstration relying party verifies an Atlas identity from a published cryptographic key, with no shared secret and no API call to Atlas required to trust the result.

Apps on four platforms

macOS, iOS, Android and Windows apps carry a person's identity and, optionally, a declared connection origin, with invited testers.

Rules locked on your devices

Since September 27, 2026, the apps encrypt a person's routing rules on their own devices before saving them. Atlas stores only the encrypted copy and cannot read which sites the rules name or where they send them.

An optional declared origin

A person can choose where their connection is seen as originating from, confirmed independently by third-party systems and not enabled by default.

Self-held recovery

A one-time phrase generated at signup and never held by Atlas is the only way to recover or re-key an account.

No link from account to exit traffic

The exit a device connects through admits it by an identity-issued credential and keeps no record of which account used which network address. This is live on the running exits, not only a design intent.

What is not yet built: a public sign-up (registration is closed and curated), a second independently-operated relying party, and the final cryptographic step described in Section 5.

03

What Atlas Commits To

These are structural choices, not a privacy policy that could be quietly revised. Each one gives something up in exchange for not being able to be compelled to give up more.

No company-held recovery

The recovery phrase is generated once, shown once, and held only by the person. Atlas keeps no copy and has no override. The accepted cost: if a person loses the phrase too, Atlas cannot get the account back for them.

Identity and address are kept apart

No record Atlas keeps joins a person's identity directly to a network address they have used. Authorizing a connection requires holding a valid credential — it does not require Atlas to know, or ask, who is behind it.

Public-key verification, not shared secrets

Every identity assertion Atlas issues is signed and can be checked against a key Atlas publishes. A relying party verifies it directly instead of trusting an API response or a secret passed out of band.

Closed, individually approved relying parties

Atlas is not an open standard anyone can integrate against unannounced. Every relying party is approved one at a time, so its cryptographic setup is confirmed in advance rather than trusted blind.

Unlinkable by design, per service

The identifier one service sees for a person cannot be matched to the identifier a different service sees, even if the two compare notes — the derivation exists from the first relying party onward, not bolted on later.

Rules Atlas cannot read

A person's rules open with their recovery phrase or a separate recovery code they write down, never with anything Atlas holds. The accepted cost: lose both, and the rules cannot be recovered by anyone, including Atlas.

No destination monitoring

Atlas does not inspect, log, filter, or flag which sites or services a person's traffic reaches. Policing destinations is a network provider's function; Atlas's function is proving who someone is, and it does not take on the first to make the second look safer.

04

What Atlas Can and Cannot Answer

This is the practical test of the commitments above: what Atlas could actually produce if legally compelled to disclose everything it holds. The honest answer is a mix — some things are recorded because the service cannot work otherwise, and a larger set of things simply are not recorded, because nothing in the design writes them down.

QuestionAnswerable?
Does an email address belong to an Atlas account, and when was it created?Yes
Which devices are enrolled to an account, and each device's current network address?Yes — current state only, not history beyond a short retention window
Which optional connection origin has an account currently chosen?Yes — the current choice, not a history of past choices
How many connection credentials did an account request in a period?Yes — a count, not which credentials
Which person was behind a given network address at a past point in time?No — no record exists beyond the short retention window
Which sites do a person's routing rules name, and where do they send them?No, for rules locked by the current apps. Atlas holds only an encrypted copy it cannot open. Rules saved by an app from before September 27, 2026 stay readable until that person's app updates.
What destinations or sites did a person's traffic reach?No — Atlas keeps no flow logs or per-person destination logs
Which person does a given relying-party identifier belong to, using only what the relying party holds?No — the identifier is unlinkable without Atlas's own signing material
Can Atlas revoke a device or an account on request?Yes — this exists and is used

Atlas cannot offer a warrant-gated exception to any "No" above without converting it back into a "Yes" for everyone — there is no partial version of not keeping a record.

05

Where This Is a Rule, Not Yet a Guarantee

One item above is honestly in between. The unlinkable per-service identifier already holds: no relying party, alone or comparing notes with another, can identify a person from the identifier it holds. But Atlas's own issuing system, today, is capable of computing which person a given identifier belongs to, because it is the system that signed it in the first place — the question "is this identifier person X?" is currently one Atlas could be asked to answer.

The rule in place until that changes: Atlas's issuing system stores nothing about a credential after handing it out except a count. No credential value, no hash, and no linking key is written down anywhere. That makes the question above unanswerable in practice today, but it rests on a written rule about what the system chooses not to store, not on a mathematical property of the cryptography.

The remaining engineering step — a blind-signature scheme, reviewed by an outside cryptographer before it ships — converts that rule into a property: Atlas will become structurally unable to compute the answer at all, not merely committed not to look. That step gates Atlas opening beyond invited testers. This page will be updated when it ships.
